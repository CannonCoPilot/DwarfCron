The Wildlife Backlog

Thirteen candidates for versions after the planned run ends at v6.1. Each one says what is already measured, so the next planning pass can tell the items that are ready to build from the ones that still need a rig night or a decision. Nothing here is scheduled.

Decided · applies now

A managed cavern raid is an irruption. The word "raid" belongs to Dwarf Fortress's own invasion machinery, which this tool never touches; what the tool does is raise pressure on a cavern layer until a wave arrives angry. Irruption is the ecological term for exactly that — a population surging out of its usual range — and it keeps the tool's vocabulary honest about what it is doing. The v6.1 package is renamed Cavern pressure and irruptions, and the term carries through the plugin's console, its guide and both reports.

Changed by measurement · 18 September

The cavern layer was never actually managed, and the lever that reaches it is the raws. Everything the tool does to a population entry — the seasonal roster, the per-layer ceiling, the whole cavern design — was written against a mechanism that does not operate underground. Measured directly: with the tool switched off and all 109 managed local entries held at zero for 12,071 ticks, and separately all 914 cave master entries, the entries stayed at zero and cavern life kept arriving at full rate. Zeroing a cavern entry does not gate a cavern wave. creature_raw.frequency does, totally and reversibly. Two consequences land here: the per-layer cavern ceiling has to be rebuilt on that lever rather than on entry quantity, and v6.1 irruptions cannot work as described above — raising pressure by opening entries does nothing down there, so the package needs redesigning around frequency before it is planned further. A second correction came with it: half of what the tool counted as cavern wildlife was the magma sea and the underworld — magma crabs and demons kilometres below an untroubled fort — which is now excluded from the count.

The answer is already in hand

Five items where the measurement exists and the remaining work is code. These could be picked up in any version.

Measured

Frequency as the balance lever

Use each creature's FREQUENCY to tune how often it is drawn, instead of only opening and closing entries.

What we know
The token does two separate jobs: it places a species' distribution epicentre on the world map, and it sets the odds at a wave draw — the game picks a species from the region's list, then rolls a d100 against its frequency. That second role is why armed predators are scarce on the rig: a dingo is FREQUENCY:5 and fails nineteen rolls in twenty, which is most of why E18's packs arrived late and why only one large-predator group holds a map at a time. The tool already rewrites a raw in memory and restores it — that is how pack sizes have worked since v5.5.
Measured, 18 September 2026 — and it is stronger than we thought
The experiment happened, and frequency turned out to be the lever the cavern layer needs, not just a surface refinement. E34: seven common cavern species set to frequency 0 produced zero arrivals across two suppressed half-seasons, while 27 and 39 other cavern animals arrived beside them; restored at the half-way tick they came straight back (28 and 15); and an untouched control had those same species arriving freely in the same window (29 and 43), which rules out a timing effect. So an in-memory frequency change is read at the draw, exactly as cluster_number is. A raw edit also cannot outlive the world — set, unload, reload, and the originals read back — so a crash cannot leave a player's world altered.
⚠️ One creature will freeze the game, and the fix is trivial
Never write frequency 0. Write 1. Zeroing CREEPY_CRAWLER — on its own, with nothing else touched — freezes Dwarf Fortress silently and unrecoverably: no crash, no log entry, the game log stops growing and the process has to be killed. It is the only creature in the raws carrying VERMIN_ROTTER with any underground range; the other four rotters are surface-only. Removing the last underground rotter is what breaks it, not the number of species suppressed — thirteen assorted cavern species can be zeroed with no effect at all. Setting that same creature to 1 instead of 0 runs at full speed, and 1 against a typical 50 or 100 is suppression for every practical purpose. The rule costs nothing and removes the whole class of hazard without needing to know which creature is load-bearing on a given world.
What it would take
A per-species frequency override in the roster, written at the same moment as the cluster size and restored with the pool, never writing a bare zero. Still open before a balance rule can be written: whether frequency is an independent per-species probability or a comparative weight competing for a shared spawn budget — the wiki says comparative, and E34 hinted at it, since the other cavern species fell 39 → 0 and 27 → 12 across the restore while the control's held steady. If it is comparative, raising one species quietly lowers every other, and a roster sets proportions rather than rates.
Measured

Population number needs no adjustment

The tool is already editing exactly this number; only the wording around it is wrong.

What we know
The token sets how many of a creature live in each world-map tile of the region, not how many may ever visit a fortress. That regional stock is precisely what the tool's quantity control writes, and E15 showed the game debits it exactly, at every size, when it draws a wave. So there is nothing to compensate for.
What it would take
Wording, not code. The roster should say regional stock where it now implies a per-fort budget, so a player reading "quantity 100" understands it as the region's herd rather than a hundred animals queued to visit.
Measured

Solitary, pack or herd for every creature

Scan the whole creature list once and publish the grouping table, with overrides.

What we know
v5.7 already labels a group from its flags — a flier is a flock, a LARGE_PREDATOR caste a pack, anything else a herd — and T5 proved the raws' cluster size is not a safe gate: a cougar is CLUSTER_NUMBER:1:1 yet the coupling had made a pack of six, which went unled until the rule stopped deferring to the raws. The full token reference, all 377 of them, is already in the repository.
What it would take
One pass over every creature combining cluster size, population number and the behaviour flags, emitted as a reviewable table with a per-species override — the same shape the ecology classes already use for their unclassified review list.
Measured

The largest male leads

Pick a group's leader by body size and sex rather than by the lowest unit id.

What we know
E28 settled that a leader is the lever that holds a group together — mean spread of five tiles against ten untouched, with the pack still roaming as far — and the current rule picks the lowest id purely because it is stable and re-picks cleanly when the leader leaves. Body size already sits on the caste, and the tool reads it as adultSize for the predation model.
What it would take
A comparator, plus a tie-break for groups that are all female or single-caste, and a re-pick rule that does not thrash when two animals are the same size. Small, and it would make the choice visible in the status line.
Measured

Concurrency from the size of the embark

Let the number of groups on the map scale with the map, rather than sitting at three.

What we know
The cap is a fixed three today. The proposal — the square root of the embark's tile count, plus one — gives five on a four-by-four, three on a two-by-two and seven on a six-by-six, which is the right direction. The site's tile bounds are already read at load for biome detection. E21 matters here: one scheduler serves the surface and cavern layers without starving either, so raising the cap is a question of density, not fairness.
What it would take
One expression and a player override, plus a re-read when the cap changes mid-game. Worth a season on a large embark to see whether the fort's frame rate minds.

One experiment away

Four items where a specific, designed test would settle the question. Each names the test.

Needs a test

Deep water is a property of the map, not the species

Keep deep-ocean creatures rare unless the embark actually has deep tiles.

What we know
The split cannot key off biome: the raws carry only OCEAN_ARCTIC, OCEAN_TEMPERATE and OCEAN_TROPICAL, with no deep or shallow variant. It has to key off the map, and E25 already measures the thing that matters — water depth relative to the land-entry surface. Two ocean forts differ sharply on it: one has open water two to three levels below the surface, the other's nearest deep water is thirty-nine levels down, which is why the detector calls the second dormant.
The test
Classify each ocean species by the depth of water it is found in at embark across the ocean and lake forts, then gate the deep ones on the map holding deep tiles at all. The survey is a single pass; the gate is then a filter on the roster.
Needs a test

Migrants are not broken

The forcing triggers do nothing useful; the migration itself works.

What we know
E22 found that force Migrants twice and migrants-now twice all returned success and not one migrant reached the map in 36,000 ticks, with the population stuck at seven. That looked like a broken feature. T6 then ran the lake fort for a full year without touching any trigger and the fort grew from seven citizens to eighteen. So migration is healthy and arrives on the game's own schedule; what fails is the forcing, inside a trial window.
The test
A narrower question now: what does the trigger actually schedule, and is there a wealth or population gate in front of it. Run a fort a year with the trigger and a year without, and compare when waves land rather than whether they land.
Needs a test

Birds already perch

Before adding a lever, find out whether more perching is even wanted.

What we know
This one is measured in the opposite direction from the way it was asked. Ravens sit still for five to twenty thousand ticks in every arm of E28, T4b and T5 — so consistently that the v5.7 stuck sweep had to exempt fliers outright, or it would have spent the season dragging perched birds out of trees.
The test
Sample the perched fraction by species across a season and see what it really is. Only then decide whether a lever is wanted, and whether the complaint is about time perched or about where they perch.
Needs a test

The creature that showed as r2 and r4

Identify it, then decide whether it matters.

What we know
Nothing in the run data names it. The rig reads the screen by glyph, and a lowercase r is the game's rodent tile, so the likely reading is a rodent-class creature appearing in groups of two and four — which would make the digits a group count, not part of a name.
The test
Cheap and opportunistic: the next time it appears, probe the units on the map and match the glyph against each creature's tile in the raws. No dedicated run needed.

Open questions

Four items that need a decision or a mechanism before any test would mean anything. These are the expensive ones.

Open

Grouping creatures by where on Earth they belong

Marsupials with marsupials, old world with old world — and arctic never with antarctic.

Why it is hard
The game's raws carry no continent, realm or hemisphere token. Biome tokens describe climate, not geography, which is why a temperate forest in one corner of a world is indistinguishable from one on the far side. The arctic-versus-antarctic case is the sharpest: there is only OCEAN_ARCTIC, and no southern equivalent at all, so the distinction the request asks for does not exist in the data to be read out of it.
The routes
Three, none cheap. Creature classes carry some signal. Name and clade heuristics carry a little more, and the tool already does clade detection for its ecology classes. The honest route is probably a curated realm table shipped with the tool, which makes this a content project rather than a code one — hundreds of species assigned by hand, then a roster filter that prefers within-realm partners. Worth scoping before committing.
Open

Quieting animal-on-animal combat

Keep the fights in the log, out of the player's face.

Why it is hard
The game's announcement configuration filters by event type, not by who is fighting. Every combat line is written as [COMBAT_*:A_D:UCR], where the first flag sends it to the on-screen log and the second ties it to the unit combat report. There is no participant filter anywhere in that file, so no edit to it can separate two dingoes from a dwarf and a dingo.
The routes
A tool-side filter is the only way: read the reports the game is accumulating and suppress those whose participants are all non-citizens, leaving the game's own log file untouched. That is mechanically possible and needs a decision first — suppressing reports means a player loses the record on screen, and a wildlife kill near the fort is sometimes exactly what they wanted to see.
Open

A record of who eats whom, over time

Turn the fights into a species-interaction history the player can look at.

Why it is hard
Not technically hard — the rig already does most of it. Every experiment writes a combat table naming the attacker, the victim and the text, and the tally that attributes a kill to its killer was written for T4 and reused for every run since. The game also writes its own log continuously, which is the source a shipped tool would have to parse instead.
The routes
A parser for the combat lines, a rolling tally per species pair, and somewhere to show it. The real question is where it belongs: this is the most product-shaped item on the list and it wants the window that v6.0 builds, so it should follow rather than lead. It also pairs naturally with the item above — the same filter that decides what to quiet decides what to count.
Open

Placement is even; survival is not

The one debt the water layer shipped with, carried here so it is not lost.

Why it is hard
The water job places round-robin, one animal per species in turn, and E33 showed that still drifts: tigerfish finished as five of eleven in both replicates of a season. Even placement does not produce an even lake, because the species do not survive at the same rate, and nothing in the placement rule can see that.
The routes
Weight the next placement by what is actually swimming rather than by what was last placed — a stocking target per species instead of one target for the layer. That needs a view on what the right balance is, which is really the same question the geographic grouping above asks, one layer down.
